# 28 — Cooling and Device Anomaly Detection

_Detect cooling faults with physics, not prompts._

The detector to build is a deterministic, layered fault-detection system, and the LLM belongs only at the end as an optional explainer. Three layers do the detecting: per-signal data-quality checks, operating-state-gated physics rules in the style of ASHRAE Guideline 36, and residual-plus-accumulator statistics (EWMA/CUSUM against regressed baselines). A topology-aware correlator then turns many symptoms into one incident with a ranked root. This is how production HVAC fault detection works today: fixed thresholds in the unit controller and BMS remain the hard alarm, and rule libraries plus "expected-behaviour" models sit above them as a separate early-warning stream that people triage. It is also what the benchmarks support, since simple statistical detectors match or beat deep learning and LLMs trail even ARIMA on numeric anomaly detection. The dcim-platform already has most of the plumbing. The taxonomy has a reserved `detection=derived` slot for analytics, the ingest worker has a throttled-sweep pattern guarded by advisory locks, rollups run to `telemetry_1d` and are kept forever, `forecast.py` contains an OLS/Holt-Winters/MAD toolkit with no numpy, and `thermal.py` already holds hot-spot and CRAH verdicts. Two gaps block a credible build. First, nothing maps racks to the CRAH that actually cools them. Second, the simulator can only fail abruptly: it cannot foul a condenser, clog a filter gradually or drift a sensor, and those slow faults are exactly where anomaly detection earns its keep over thresholds. The plan below closes both gaps first, then ships the detector in six phases, each gated by event-level scoring against injected faults.

Throughout, three kinds of statement are kept apart. **Sourced** facts carry a link. **Vendor claims** are labelled as such, because no independent accuracy evaluation of any commercial datacenter cooling anomaly product was found. **[Judgement]** marks engineering practice or design recommendations that no fetched source in this research tied down. Codebase references use `P:` for `dcim-platform/` and `S:` for `Datacenter_Network_Simulator/`, with the simulator abbreviations DSS (`core/device_state_store.py`), CM (`core/cooling_model.py`), BPG (`core/bacnet_plant_generator.py`) and BC (`simulator/bacnet_controller.py`).

## Real datacenters stack thresholds, rules and a quiet advisory tier

Production cooling fault detection is overwhelmingly rule-based. The canonical rule sets are NIST's APAR, a set of "simple rules" derived from mass and energy balances that caught faults such as an outdoor-air damper stuck open in field data ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/publications/expert-rule-set-fault-detection-air-handling-units)), and its descendant, the ASHRAE Guideline 36 AHU fault conditions. G36 routines "continually assess AHU performance by comparing the values of BAS inputs and outputs to a subset of potential fault conditions". The subset depends on the **operating state**, and **time delays are applied to evaluation and reporting to suppress false alarms** ([ASHRAE G36-2021 Addendum p](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/g36_2021_p_20240229.pdf)). The rule equations themselves keep being corrected: addendum p fixed single-zone VAV FC#3 and FC#9, and a 2026 addendum to G36-2024 fixed FC#4 and FC#5 ([G36-2024 Addendum e](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/g36_2024_e_20260916.pdf)). Any rule set we build therefore needs version numbers. No datacenter-specific equivalent of G36 for CRAHs, CDUs or chiller plants was found. The G36 rule *form* (state-gated inequality, sensor tolerance band, persistence delay) carries over directly, but the specific datacenter rules below are [judgement]. ASHRAE TC 9.9 supplies the envelope, not fault rules: 18–27 °C recommended at IT inlet, A1 allowable 15–32 °C, and a high-density H1 class with a 22 °C recommended and 25 °C allowable upper limit ([Upsite on the 5th edition](https://www.upsite.com/blog/what-you-need-to-know-about-ashraes-fifth-edition-of-thermal-guidelines/); [Upsite on H1](https://www.upsite.com/blog/major-changes-to-ashraes-fifth-edition-of-thermal-guidelines-part-2-new-air-cooled-class-for-high-density-compute-equipment)).

Commercial products layer on top of this, and almost every capability claim is the vendor's own. Honeywell Forge advertises **200+ manufacturer-agnostic rules** for CRACs, CRAHs, chillers and VFDs ([Honeywell](https://www.honeywell.com/us/en/press/2022/09/honeywell-launches-new-suite-of-solutions-to-help-optimize-data-center-uptime-and-productivity)). EkkoSense's v9.4 (March 2026) explicitly targets assets "that have not yet hit the threshold for a BMS alarm", using drift from control setpoint ([IT Brief](https://itbrief.com.au/story/ekkosense-adds-early-anomaly-alerts-for-hybrid-cooling)). Vertiv Next Predict (January 2026) sells "AI-based anomaly detection" as a managed service in which Vertiv staff do the root-cause work ([Vertiv](https://www.vertiv.com/en-us/about/news-and-insights/corporate-news/vertiv-announces-new-ai-powered-predictive-maintenance-service-for-modern-data-centers-and-ai-factories/)). Schneider EcoStruxure IT documents one predictive feature, UPS battery end-of-life, and **bundles alarms into incidents every 5 minutes "to prevent alarm storms"** ([EcoStruxure IT](https://ecostruxureit.com/?p=23657)). The pattern these share is the design signal: the anomaly tier is a separate early-warning or degradation stream that fires *below* BMS thresholds and goes to people, not to the pager.

Closed-loop ML is rare and is a different problem. Google/DeepMind's system snapshots thousands of sensors every five minutes, discards low-confidence actions, has the local control system re-verify every action, and falls back to on-site rules. Google reports about 30% kW/ton savings over nine months ([Google](https://blog.google/inside-google/infrastructure/safety-first-ai-autonomous-data-center-cooling-and-industrial-control/)). Phaidra claims about 80% fewer AI-workload thermal spikes in NVIDIA tests (vendor claim, [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/backed-by-nvidia-seattles-phaidra-targets-data-center-overheating-with-proactive-ai)). Both are optimisation and control, not fault detection, and both are hyperscale or well-funded pilots.

The evidence that any of this works comes from commercial buildings, not datacenters. LBNL's study of more than 60,000 HVAC units across six FDD vendors found **about 40% of AHUs reporting at least one fault on any given day** and 18 fault types persisting more than 20% of the period ([LBNL](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/bibcite/reference/36129)). Fault findings plainly go unactioned at scale. FDD users saw a **median 9% annual energy saving** after two or more years ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/articles/smart-energy-analytics-campaign-reveals-continued-energy-and-cost-savings)). LBNL still says "considerable uncertainties remain regarding the accuracy and effectiveness" of FDD products ([LBNL](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/performance-evaluation-framework)). No published lead-time or false-positive rate exists for any datacenter cooling product. That makes the evaluation harness in this design a deliverable in its own right, not a nicety. On stakes, Uptime attributes 45% of impactful outages to power in its 2025 analysis ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/a/5239951)). Cooling sits second, at roughly 13–19% depending on survey edition ([FOSS Force on the 2024 survey](https://fossforce.com/2024/08/power-issues-cause-most-data-center-outages-followed-by-cooling/)), and its forgiveness margin shrinks as density rises.

## Seven layers map onto seams the platform already has

The recommended architecture separates detection by speed and by what each layer can honestly claim. Each layer feeds the next, and only layers 1 and 2 may raise alarms on their own authority.

| Layer | What it does | Runs where | Emits | Platform seam |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L0 Data quality | Stale, dropout, flatline-while-process-moves, spike, out-of-physical-range, quality≠GOOD | Detector sweep | Sensor-health findings; masks series for L2–L4 | `quality` column, `min_valid`/`max_valid` (`worker.py:775-788`), staleness sweep (`P:backend/app/alarms/staleness.py`) |
| L1 Hard thresholds and device bits | Envelope, high or low limits, fault bits, traps | Ingest (exists) | Alarms | `alarm_rule` + `engine.py:219-315`, trap path, polled backstop |
| L2 State-gated FDD rules | G36-style inequalities with tolerance band and dwell, one diagnosis per rule | Detector sweep, 60 s | `detection=derived` alerts (MINOR/WARNING) plus findings | `raise_alarm(source="analytics")` (`repositories/alarms.py:217-376`) |
| L3 Residual + accumulator | Measured minus expected (regression, curve, seasonal profile), normalised, fed to EWMA/CUSUM | Detector sweep, 60–300 s; baselines nightly | Advisory findings | New `anomaly_*` tables; baselines from `telemetry_1h`/`1d` |
| L4 Peer and spatial | Robust z (median/MAD) across same-model siblings, aisle-level inlet residuals; later per-class PCA SPE | Detector sweep, 300 s | Advisory findings; sensor-vs-process discrimination | Room membership (`repositories/thermal.py:54-56`) |
| L5 Correlation and root cause | Group findings and alarms by cooling chain and time; rank the root; suppress child symptoms | On finding open or change | Incidents | Extends `alarms/correlation.py` |
| L6 Explainer (optional) | Narrative plus ranked hypotheses from a deterministic evidence bundle | On incident open or request | Labelled AI text | New, pluggable, off by default |

The split between L2 and L3 is the most important design decision. An L2 rule names a fault and its expected physical consequence. An example: "CRAH supply is 3 K over setpoint for 15 min while its CHW valve is at or above 95% and the unit is running, so it is capacity-starved." That rule is explainable, transfers between sites, and works on day one with no history. APAR's "robust" parameters were developed precisely to remove site-specific tuning ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/689426)). An L3 residual says "this chiller's COP is 2.1σ below what its load and wet-bulb predict, and the CUSUM has been accumulating for 9 hours". That catches what no threshold sees, but it needs a baseline and it does not diagnose. Treating the two outputs differently in routing is what keeps the console honest.

The choice of algorithms inside L3 and L4 rests on benchmark evidence, not fashion. Schmidl et al. re-implemented 71 algorithms over 976 series and found that "deep learning approaches are not (yet) competitive" and that "simple methods yield performance almost as good as more sophisticated methods". They also found **trend anomalies the hardest class (average AUC-ROC below 0.6)** and point extrema the easiest ([Schmidl et al., PVLDB 2022](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol15/p1779-wenig.pdf)). Cooling's dominant slow failures are trend anomalies: fouling, refrigerant loss, filter loading and sensor drift. Spike detectors will therefore miss them, and an accumulating statistic on a residual is mandatory. CUSUM-style accumulation is the standard fix for slow-drift faults where plain variance statistics fail on the Tennessee Eastman benchmark ([ADCHEM 2018](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/ADCHEM-2018/media/files/0146.pdf)). TSB-AD (1,070 series, 40 algorithms) found that "more than half" of the top univariate methods are statistical and that PCA-family methods rank at or near the top for multivariate data ([Liu & Paparrizos, NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/c3f3c690b7a99fba16d0efd35cb83b2c-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf)). Time-series foundation models fare worse under scrutiny. An ICLR 2026 study found their anomaly scores "do not significantly differ" from moving-window variance and squared-difference baselines ([ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10010437)), so Chronos- or TimesFM-class models are not justified at 716 devices. Isolation Forest-style tree methods do well on point extrema ([Schmidl](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol15/p1779-wenig.pdf)) and add little here. They would also pull numpy, scipy and sklearn onto a 7.7 GB host.

The cadence follows the data. BACnet plant points poll every 30 s, Modbus every 30 s, `snmp-sensor` every 10 s, Redfish every 60 s plus push, and server, PDU and probe SNMP every 120 s (`poll_profile` migrations 0003, 0010, 0022, 0027, 0054/0055). A 60 s sweep is therefore the natural L2 tick. It can see valve hunting with a period of minutes on 30 s BACnet data, but not on 120 s SNMP, which aliases it [judgement]. Fast liquid-cooling transients are out of reach for any platform-side detector: rack inlets reach 35 °C within 25–45 s of airflow loss at 5–20 kW per cabinet ([Upsite, citing Khankari/ASHRAE](https://www.upsite.com/?p=3601)), and liquid ride-through is shorter still. Those belong to the controller and the L1 hard alarm. The detector's job is minutes-to-weeks.

## Sensor faults must be ruled out before process faults are scored

The single biggest source of false cooling anomalies is a bad sensor, so L0 runs first and masks what it condemns. The principle is analytical redundancy. A sensor fault breaks only its own variable's relationships, while a process fault propagates to physically linked variables. In PCA terms, if one variable dominates the squared prediction error (more than about 50%), rebuild the model without it. If the remaining variables fall back within limits, the fault is in the sensor ([AIChE 2005](https://folk.ntnu.no/skoge/prost/proceedings/aiche-2005/non-topical/Non%20topical/papers/402c.pdf)). PolyU's work argues that slow bias and drift are the hardest sensor faults, that model-based methods struggle to separate them from equipment degradation, and that steady-state energy and mass conservation residuals are the fix for chilled-water temperature sensors and flow meters ([PolyU](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/robust-sensor-fault-diagnosis-and-validation-in-hvac-systems/)).

Concretely, L0 has six checks, all [judgement] in their parameters. The first is **dropout**: no sample within 3× the poll interval. This reuses the staleness grace logic (`staleness.py:36-56`) and treats "data unavailable" as distinct from "value 0". The second is **quality**: any sample not GOOD is excluded from scoring. Ingest already stores out-of-range gauges as `quality='suspect'` rather than dropping them (`worker.py:775-788`), and a run of suspect samples is itself a sensor-health finding. The third is **physical range**, for example −40 °C as a disconnected-thermistor sentinel. The fourth is **spike**, using a Hampel filter of rolling median ± 5·MAD. The fifth is **flatline-while-process-moves**: zero variance over N samples *conditioned on* a coupled variable moving. A frozen CRAH supply temperature while its valve swings is stuck, while a frozen setpoint is normal. The sixth is **bias and drift**: CUSUM on a redundancy residual. Two forms of redundancy exist here. Peer redundancy compares rack inlet probes in the same aisle. Balance redundancy compares CRAH air-side Q = ṁ·cp·(T_return − T_supply) with water-side Q. A sustained mismatch beyond roughly 10–15% points at a sensor or flow meter, not the process [judgement]. A physical-bound rule catches mislabelled or misplaced probes cheaply. A rack inlet colder than its CRAH supply minus a tolerance is a sensor or mapping error, and a probe reading 10–15 K high consistently is probably in the hot aisle [judgement].

Two simulator-specific traps matter for this layer. SNMP values in the simulator are file-backed, and only OIDs that the simulator patches actually move. A naive flatline check would therefore flag every unpatched static point as stuck. The "conditioned on the process moving" clause handles this, and L0 also needs a per-metric `expect_variation` flag, sourced from the metric registry or a detector config [judgement]. Separately, booleans are stored only on change plus a 900 s heartbeat, so readers must look back 1800 s (`P:backend/app/ingest/changelog.py:48,52`). Duty-cycle and starts-per-hour features (CH-4, PU-3) must be rebuilt with last-known-value logic, not by counting rows.

Every L3 and L4 finding should carry a `kind` of `sensor`, `process` or `undetermined`. Where both a peer residual and a balance residual exist, the classification rule is: only one sensor's relations broken means sensor; linked variables moving consistently means process. Only `process` findings feed the cascade correlator as candidate roots. Sensor findings go to a maintenance queue with their own count, so sensor health becomes a visible metric rather than a hidden source of noise.

## One chiller trip should produce one incident, not 200 rack alerts

The physics of a cooling cascade is well characterised. The plant-side signal comes first: chiller status, CHW supply slope, then CRAH valves saturating. Room supply follows, then rack inlet, then fleet-wide server fan ramp, then throttling. In one case report, temperature rose about 2 °C per minute after cooling loss and areas exceeded 40 °C within 15 minutes ([ES Magazine](https://www.esmagazine.com/articles/95847-the-heat-is-on-but-the-cracs-are-off-now-what)). With pumps and CRAH fans on UPS and a chilled-water storage tank, a power outage can cause very little temperature excursion ([Schneider](https://blog.se.com/datacenter/architecture/2014/09/25/ensure-data-center-cooling-system-can-ride-power-failure)). The simulator reproduces this chain with realistic lags. The CHW penalty settles with τ ≈ 16 s and runs away at 0.20 K/s, unpowered units get 60 s of ride-through (DSS:4811-4826), stopped-CRAH discharge soaks toward return air with τ = 240 s (BPG:205-259), and server CPU temperature lags with τ = 150 s (DSS:7251-7316). The correlator's job is to recognise that shape and collapse it.

The platform's existing correlation does not cover cooling. `correlate` (`P:backend/app/alarms/correlation.py:378-429`) applies only to reachability and link types, walks management → fieldbus → power over at most 2 hops, and marks symptoms for display. A rack-inlet anomaly today would not be linked to its CRAH or chiller. L5 should be a new correlator over a **cooling dependency graph** with five tiers: chiller/tower/pump train → CHW loop → CRAH or CDU → zone (rack set) → server. Its algorithm is [judgement]. When a finding or alarm opens, walk upstream to the nearest tier that has its own open process finding or alarm within a ±10 min window. Make that the incident root, attach the others as symptoms (`is_symptom`, `root_cause_alarm_id` already exist on `alarm`), and rank competing roots by tier height then onset time. A power-chain hop is added through the existing `/power/chain` and `/topology/impact` services (`services/power.py:236`; `services/impact.py:27-38`), so an RPP trip that unpowers a CRAH becomes the root of the cooling incident it causes. Expected-state context suppresses false roots: a CRAH stopped by the fire panel's gas pre-discharge, a unit in standby rotation, or a device inside a `maintenance_window` (`0046`) is explained, not anomalous.

The graph is only partly there, and this is the first missing piece. The `connection` table's `cooling` layer holds hydronic edges: header → CRAH/CDU → return (`S:core/fleet_lifecycle.py:1588-1590`), and CDU ↔ cold-plate server (`S:tools/fill_cold_plate_loops.py:210-216`). On the liquid side the graph is therefore complete enough. On the air side, **no CRAH→rack "serves" edge exists**. CRAHs join racks only by shared room (`P:backend/app/repositories/thermal.py:54-56`), and the simulator itself has all 7 CRAHs in a hall serve every rack in it (DSS:1579-1580). The CHW loop also has no explicit entity and must be inferred from cooling edges. Without a rack-to-CRAH mapping, L5 can say "Hall A cooling" but not "CRAH-A-03's zone".

The realistic fix is the one doc 27 already plans as D9: a fitted **influence matrix**. For each room, regress each rack's inlet on each CRAH's supply temperature and running state, using CRAH trips and staging as natural experiments, and label the result as an estimate. EkkoSense markets the same idea as "Zones of Influence", an ML clustering that is continuously re-fitted (cited in `P:docs/27-digital-twin-plan.md:126-127`, vendor description). This is the one place in the design where a learned model earns its keep. It is ordinary ridge or least-squares regression, writable in pure Python for 7 CRAHs × ~40 racks per hall, and it needs no deep learning. Two preconditions apply. The simulator must run `DCIM_SIM_AIR_MODEL=spatial`, because in the default `uniform` mode every hall rack sees the same supply plus white noise and no influence structure exists to learn (DSS:878-879). The fit also needs about 4+ weeks of history (doc 27 Phase 5). Until then, L5 falls back to room-level grouping. As a bridge, a static distance prior (exp(−d/5 m), the same kernel the simulator's air model uses, `S:core/air_model.py:128-134`) can be computed from imported floor geometry, if the importer stores CRAH and rack x/y. That was not line-verified.

## Advisories live in their own queue under ISA-18.2 budgets

Alarm-management standards give the principled reason to keep anomalies out of the alarm stream. EEMUA 191 benchmarks a long-term steady-state average of **fewer than 1 alarm per 10 minutes per operator**, fewer than 10 in the first 10 minutes after a major upset, and a priority split of about **5% high, 15% medium, 80% low** ([ABB white paper](https://library.e.abb.com/public/72f20c70c7b44d889d463db81df5c38d/SCADA%20Alarm%20Management%20White%20Paper.pdf)). About 6 alarms per hour per operator is described as "very likely acceptable" ([open-exam-prep ISA-18.2 guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/isa-cap/system-design-hmi-alarms-enterprise/alarm-management-isa182)). ISA-18.2 treats anything that does not require a timely operator response as not an alarm. A COP drifting down over a week requires a work order, not a response within minutes, so by definition it is an advisory.

The routing therefore has three lanes, all [judgement] in their thresholds:

| Lane | Contents | Storage | Visible in | Pages / tickets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alarm | L1 hard thresholds, device bits, traps; MAJOR/CRITICAL | `alarm` (exists) | Alarm list, home strip, websocket | Yes, Jira policy MAJOR+ alarm class, 300 s age (`P:backend/app/integrations/policy.py:45-99`) |
| Alert (derived) | L2 FDD rules with a named diagnosis; MINOR/WARNING; `detection=derived` | `alarm` via `raise_alarm(source="analytics")` | Alarm list filtered by detection, device alarms tab | No by default; `response_class=alert` falls outside Jira policy |
| Advisory | L3/L4 findings, sensor-health findings | New `anomaly_finding` | New triage view, device telemetry overlay | Never directly; promotion only |

Raising L2 rules through `raise_alarm` inherits dedup on `(device, alarm_type, instance)`, maintenance and commissioning shelving at raise time (`repositories/alarms.py:261-300`), device rollup, websocket fanout and the Jira outbox, all for free. Response class derives from severity, so CRITICAL/MAJOR become alarms and lower severities become alerts (`P:backend/app/core/alert_taxonomy.py:285-305`). The docs already record that 496 open root conditions resolve to 7 alarms and 489 alerts, so the derived lane will not flood the console. L3/L4 findings do **not** go into `alarm`, for three reasons. The alarm lifecycle (ACTIVE → CLEARED) is the wrong shape for a finding, which should persist with first-seen, last-seen, occurrence count and estimated impact. LBNL's persistence data show findings lingering for weeks ([LBNL](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/bibcite/reference/36129)), and a finding that "clears" every time the CUSUM resets is noise. Advisories must not count toward the alarm-rate KPI. And statistical findings need dismissal feedback that alarms do not have.

Promotion from advisory to alert or alarm is explicit and corroborated. An advisory becomes a derived alert when an L2 rule on the same device or chain fires within the window. It becomes MAJOR only when L5 attaches confirmed impact, for example at least N racks with RM-2 or RM-3 under the same root. Every promotion writes the finding ID into the alarm's attributes. Whether `raise_alarm` already carries arbitrary per-alarm detail fields was not verified line by line. If it does not, adding an `evidence` jsonb column on `alarm` is a one-migration change.

The advisory lane needs its own budget [judgement]: on the order of tens of new findings per day estate-wide, with each detector's precision tracked from operator feedback. Chattering (for example ≥3 raises in a short window) should be capped per series. A finding open for more than 24 h without action should be surfaced as stale rather than re-raised. A detector producing hundreds of findings per day is mis-tuned, and the fix is to tune it, not to summarise the noise. JCI's practice of attaching monetary impact to each fault is a vendor approach ([JCI](https://www.johnsoncontrols.com/insights/2024/feature-story/ai-today-autonomy-tomorrow)), but it is the right prioritisation key. A simple `impact_kw` estimate, such as extra chiller kW from a COP residual or racks at risk from a CRAH finding, sorts the queue.

## LLMs explain incidents; they never detect or actuate

The verdict is unambiguous. **The system does not need an LLM to detect anything, and must be fully functional with any LLM switched off.** The benchmark evidence is consistent. In SigLLM (MIT, 11 datasets), mean F1 was 0.753 for a deep-learning detector and 0.582 for ARIMA, against 0.525 for an LLM forecast-residual "Detector" and 0.133–0.223 for direct prompting. Detection cost $1.69–$4.30 per signal at about 5 s per window ([SigLLM, arXiv 2405.14755](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14755v3)). An ICLR 2025 study found "no evidence that they can understand more subtle real-world anomalies" ([Zhou & Yu](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.05440v3)). LLMTime found that chat-aligned models forecast worse than base models because of number tokenisation and calibration ([Gruver et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820v3)). Cooling anomalies are multivariate, physics-coupled and slow, which is the regime where these results are weakest. No benchmark on HVAC or cooling telemetry exists, and 2025–2026 frontier models have not been evaluated on SigLLM or TSB-AD. "Not proven" is the accurate status. It is not the same as "proven impossible".

"AI" in the broader sense does have three narrow, justified places in this design: the regressions behind L3 residuals, the influence-matrix fit for rack-to-CRAH zones, and, in a later phase, per-class PCA with SPE contributions. All are classical statistical learning. None needs a GPU or a deep-learning framework.

Where an LLM can add value is downstream, and the industrial evidence shows a consistent shape: deterministic collection first, LLM ranking or narration second. Microsoft's RCACopilot collects diagnostics with alert-specific handlers that ran in production for 4+ years before an LLM was added on top to predict a category and explain it, reaching up to 0.766 accuracy ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/automatic-root-cause-analysis-via-large-language-models-for-cloud-incidents)). Meta narrows candidates heuristically, then a fine-tuned 7B model ranks them, and the true cause landed in the top 5 for 42% of backtested investigations ([Meta Engineering](https://engineering.fb.com/2024/06/24/data-infrastructure/leveraging-ai-for-efficient-incident-response/)). On hard benchmarks, LLM agents get the exact root-cause set right only about 4–21% of the time and show "hallucinated data interpretation" at every capability tier ([arXiv 2602.09937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.09937v1); [OpenRCA 2.0](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2606.27154)). Siemens and JCI both put GenAI on top of existing analytics engines, and JCI pre-constructs prompts rather than exposing free chat ([Facilities Dive](https://www.facilitiesdive.com/news/johnson-controls-openblue-generative-ai-upgrades-building-automation-system/732802/)). LLM output is therefore decision support, displayed as "candidates to check".

The guarded explainer pattern is [judgement] assembled from those systems and contains six controls. First, the LLM is **invoked per incident**, on open, on escalation or on operator request, never per sample and never in the alarm path. The alarm fires before, and regardless of, the LLM. Second, its input is a **deterministic evidence bundle**. Every item carries an ID: the alert (rule, expected versus observed, onset), correlated series *summaries* (min, max and slope over a window, never raw series), topology edges from L5 (`T1: CHW-LOOP-2 → CRAH-B-03`), recent setpoint or override changes, maintenance windows, similar past incidents, and OEM or SOP snippets via retrieval. Third, its output must follow a **JSON schema**: summary, hypotheses with confidence, supporting and contradicting evidence IDs, checks to perform, and unknowns. Fourth, a **validator** rejects any output that cites a missing evidence ID or names a device not in the bundle, and the system then falls back to a deterministic template summary. Fifth, **tools are read-only**: metric window, topology neighbours, runbook search. There is no write path to BACnet, Modbus, SNMP-SET, setpoints, or alarm acknowledge or suppress. CISA and partner guidance on AI in OT says automated functions must not "override necessary safety controls" ([Infosecurity Magazine summary](https://infosecurity-magazine.com/news/us-guidance-secure-ai-ot)), and a nondeterministic component cannot be credited in cooling or fire interlocks. Sixth, the system keeps an **audit trail** of the bundle, model ID, prompt and output for each incident, runs at temperature 0, and puts the panel *below* the detector evidence, labelled AI-generated, with thumbs feedback. NIST AI 600-1 lists confabulation as a named GenAI risk ([NIST](https://airc.nist.gov/docs/NIST.AI.600-1.GenAI-Profile.ipd.pdf)). Device names and ticket text enter the prompt, so they are treated as data against prompt injection.

Cost is not the constraint. At third-party-listed Claude Haiku 4.5 rates of $1 input and $5 output per million tokens ([pricepertoken](https://pricepertoken.com/pricing-page/model/anthropic-claude-haiku-4.5)), a 4k-in/600-out explanation costs about $0.007, so even 1,000 incidents per day is about $7. That arithmetic is the research notes'. Verify current pricing on Anthropic's page before budgeting. The real constraints are data egress and over-trust. Colocation, government and finance sites often forbid sending telemetry to a cloud API. The explainer should therefore be pluggable, supporting a cloud API, a local 7–8B model, or none. Meta's production ranker shows a small local model is viable for ranking tasks ([Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2024/06/24/data-infrastructure/leveraging-ai-for-efficient-incident-response/)). Two further uses carry low risk and are well accepted: drafting the Jira ticket body from the bundle, and shift-handover summaries. Natural-language querying of telemetry is acceptable only if it goes through the vetted query API, never raw SQL.

## The code already has the slots; it lacks the tables and the mapping

The detector should run as a new throttled step, `_maybe_detect_anomalies`, in the ingest worker's `_tick` loop (`P:backend/app/ingest/worker.py:238-276`). It copies the `_maybe_sweep_staleness` template (`:461-491`): a monotonic `_last_*` timer, its own `unit_of_work()`, its own error handling, outbox enqueue in the same transaction, and fanout after commit. Two ingest replicas run (`P:deploy/docker-compose.yml:113`), so the step must take a Postgres advisory lock, as the assigner (`:437-440`) and `collector_visibility.py:45` already do, so that only one replica sweeps. There is no job framework (no arq, APScheduler or Celery), and doc 02's planned arq worker was never built. A separate process would give cleaner CPU isolation on the tight host. The worker step is still the right MVP because it reuses every lifecycle hook, provided the sweep's wall time is measured. Ingest headroom under live load is unmeasured, and on 2026-10-07 deploys cut ingest from 180 to 3 ticks per minute. Scoring inside the per-sample ingest transaction is rejected for the same reason: it would put detector latency in the write path.

Data access splits by horizon. Recent scoring (L0–L2, the current window of L3) reads raw `telemetry_sample` for the last 15–30 minutes per detector class, using the `(metric_id, ts DESC)` index. `telemetry_1m` lags by up to about 2 minutes (end offset 1 m), and `device_state.metrics` holds only hot keys. `cop`, `approach_temp`, `filter_diff_pressure` and `valve_position_pct` are not hot, so they cannot come from the cache. Baselines come from `telemetry_1h` and `telemetry_1d`, which are kept forever and refit nightly. Raw data expires at 30 days and 1m data at 7 days, so any model that needs longer history must use rollups, and `sample_count` lets the fitter refuse thin buckets. SQL can do much of the baselining without Python libraries: `percentile_cont` for hour-of-week medians, `stddev_samp`, and `regr_slope`/`regr_r2` for single-regressor fits. p90/p95 are already computed this way (`estate.py:327,399,521`).

The no-numpy constraint is livable and should be kept. `P:backend/app/services/forecast.py` already implements OLS with Student-t intervals (`:161-216`), additive Holt-Winters (`:221-315`), and a MAD-scaled level-shift test (`detect_level_shift`, `:112`, flagged at 5σ). A shared `app/analytics/stats.py` should be lifted from it, adding EWMA, two-sided CUSUM, a Hampel filter, robust z and small multiple-regression OLS via normal equations. All of these are a few dozen lines each in pure Python. At ~716 devices with tens of channels at 30–120 s cadence, incremental statistics cost microseconds per point. Schmidl measured robust-PCA-class methods under 2.5 ms per point, against more than 125 ms for naive Holt-Winters and ARIMA ([Schmidl](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol15/p1779-wenig.pdf)), which argues for fitting Holt-Winters offline only. PCA for L4 (≤ 10 variables per CRAH or CDU class) is feasible in pure Python with power iteration. Isolation Forest and autoencoders are not worth the 100+ MB of dependencies. `thermal.py`'s hot-spot, high-return and supply-fault verdicts (`:41-61,132-178`) and `plant.py`'s actuator verdict become L2 rules directly. Their thresholds move into the rule catalogue, so the display verdict and the alert can never disagree.

The new schema is raw-SQL tables in an alembic migration. The project convention keeps hypertable-adjacent tables out of `models/`, and these follow it. The next migration number after the latest seen, 0108, applies.

## The simulator can trip a chiller but cannot foul one

Abrupt-fault injection is good. Plant overrides (`POST /api/bacnet/plant/overrides`, `S:api/routers/bacnet.py:590`) set any BACnet point on chillers, pumps, towers, valves, CRAHs and CDUs. `Alarm_*=1` drives coupled physics, `Unit_Running=0` is a silent stop, and a stuck valve is `ActuatorFault` plus `Position=X` (DSS:4761-4799). There is a device fault menu (`S:api/routers/devices.py:1293-1425`), metric pins, utility and ATS failure, fire smoke, and six exhaustion scenarios in `S:tools/live_campaign_exhaustion.py`. These provide ground truth for L1, L2 and L5.

What is missing is the slow failure that justifies L3. The simulator has no fouling, COP decay, gradual filter loading, pump wear or sensor drift. Rated COP is a fixed 5.5 (CM:56). `Filter_Dirty` is a single step to 0.8 delivered capacity. Tower approach is fixed at 4 K design, 1.5 K minimum (CM:422-516). There is no weather override, and wet-bulb is dry-bulb minus a fixed depression (CM:133-147). A CH-2 COP residual would therefore be flat forever, and testing it would prove nothing. A further subtlety makes naive sensor-fault injection impossible. Points the simulator publishes every tick overwrite operator values: CHW temperatures and flows, CRAH `Supply_Air_Temp`, `Return_Air_Temp` and `CHW_Valve`, and CDU `TCS_*` (DSS:6846-6849; BC:819-821). Holding a "drifted" sensor value through the override endpoint does not work for exactly the sensors that matter.

The simulator additions, in priority order, are [judgement]:

| Addition | Where | Purpose |

Exporting that weight matrix as a ground-truth endpoint would let the platform's fitted influence matrix be scored against truth. That is a rare luxury that real sites never have. Ten DC2 devices are labelled Chicago, and `city_by_dc` takes the first device seen (DSS:1570-1571), so DC2 weather may be wrong, and that would corrupt wet-bulb-gated baselines. CRAH `Alarm_HighTemp`'s +6 K coupling may also be overwritten for a running unit (DSS:6133 vs BC:819-821).

## Score events, lead time and false alerts per device-day

Evaluation must avoid the metrics that have inflated the anomaly-detection literature. Point-adjusted F1 "has a great possibility of overestimating" performance, and an untrained model scores comparably under it ([Kim et al., AAAI 2022](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/20680)). Under PA-F1 a random score ranks 26th of 33 detectors, and TSB-AD recommends VUS-PR for ranking scorers ([TSB-AD](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/c3f3c690b7a99fba16d0efd35cb83b2c-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf)). Public benchmarks are also flawed by triviality and mislabelling ([Wu & Keogh](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.13807)), and none covers datacenter cooling. The nearest analogues are RP-1043 (chiller) and SKAB (a pump-and-valve water loop) ([SKAB](https://github.com/waico/SKAB)). The simulator with its injection ledger is therefore the primary benchmark.

The scoring is event-level [judgement]. An injected fault counts as **detected** if a finding or alert of a compatible diagnosis class opens on the root device or its L5 incident between onset and onset + horizon. The horizon is 30 min for abrupt faults and the injected ramp duration for gradual ones. A finding counts as **false** if it overlaps no injection on a clean baseline run, or names the wrong root. The harness reports seven figures per detector and version: event recall; event precision; **detection delay** from onset; **lead time over L1**, meaning minutes or hours before the first hard alarm on the same chain, which is the number vendors claim but never publish; **false findings per device-day** on clean runs; **diagnosis accuracy**, meaning the right root device and the right sensor/process `kind`; and **compression ratio**, meaning symptoms absorbed per incident in cascade scenarios. VUS-PR is used only to compare scorer variants offline.

The campaign has four tiers. Unit tests run against the deterministic plant fixture (`S:tests/conftest.py` `build_plant`, pinned `_dt`; pattern from `S:tests/test_fault_campaign.py`) and synthetic series, for each detector's maths. Offline replay runs against rollups of live history with the ledger aligned, which is cheap and covers seasonality. Live campaigns run on the WSL stack in `spatial` mode. They cover abrupt faults first (the six exhaustion scenarios, stuck valve, CDU leak, CRAH stop, fire stop as a must-suppress negative control), then gradual ones as the simulator additions land. Long-run clean soaks of at least 7 days measure the false-finding rate and the advisory budget against ISA-style targets. The simulator runs in real time with no backfill, so gradual-fault campaigns take wall-clock days and should be batched. The host has suffered swapping under Timescale jobs and heavy deploys, so campaigns and deploys should not overlap.

Two acceptance gates are suggested [judgement]. L2 rules must show zero false alerts across a 7-day clean soak and detect 100% of their target abrupt faults before or with L1. L3 detectors must show at least 80% event recall on injected gradual faults at no more than 0.05 false findings per device-day, with median lead time over L1 of at least hours. Those numbers are starting targets, not sourced industry benchmarks, because no such benchmarks exist.

## Six phases deliver value before any model is fitted

| Phase | Scope | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Ground truth and plumbing** | Simulator: injection ledger, publish-layer sensor transform, seed fix, confirm `spatial` mode, check the DC2 city bug. Platform: migration for `anomaly_*` tables; `app/analytics/stats.py` lifted from `forecast.py`; `_maybe_detect_anomalies` skeleton with advisory lock and timing metrics; taxonomy entries | Sweep runs on both replicas with exactly one executing; wall time measured under live load |
| **1. L0 data quality + L2 rules** | Sensor checks; CR-1/2, CH-1/4/5, CD-1/6, VA-1/2, PU-3, RM-2/3, GN-1 via `raise_alarm(source="analytics")`; `thermal.py`/`plant.py` verdicts moved into the rule catalogue | 7-day clean soak with zero false derived alerts; all abrupt campaign scenarios detected |
| **2. L5 cooling correlator** | Cooling dependency graph from `connection` (cooling layer) + room membership + power chain; incident table; symptom suppression; fire/maintenance/standby context | Chiller-trip and hall-CRAH scenarios each produce one incident with the correct root |
| **3. L3 residuals** | Nightly baselines from `telemetry_1h`/`1d`; CH-2/3, CT-1, PU-1/2, CR-3/4, CD-2/3/5, SV-1/2, UP-1, TX-1 with EWMA/CUSUM; advisory triage view and expected-band overlay | Gradual campaigns pass the L3 gate; advisory budget held on the soak |
| **4. L4 peer, spatial and zones** | Robust z across siblings; aisle peer residuals; influence-matrix fit (doc 27 D9) scored against the simulator's air-model weights; optional per-class PCA SPE for sensor isolation | Zone attribution beats room-level grouping on CRAH-stop scenarios; sensor/process `kind` accuracy measured |
| **5. L6 explainer (optional)** | Evidence-bundle builder (useful without an LLM as a template summary); pluggable cloud, local or none explainer; validator; audit log; Jira body drafting | Validator rejection rate and operator thumbs tracked; system verified fully functional with the explainer off |

The ordering is deliberate. Phases 0–2 need no history and no fitted models, which means they work on day one at any site. That is the cold-start answer APAR's robust parameters gave for rule layers ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/689426)). Phase 3 needs 2–4 weeks of history for hour-of-week baselines [judgement]. Phase 4 needs 4+ weeks plus spatial mode. Phase 5 is last because every input it consumes is built in the earlier phases. The deterministic evidence bundle is valuable on its own as the alert-detail panel even if no LLM is ever attached. Per the deploy-batching lesson, each phase should ship as one deploy, with Redis stream lag (`XINFO GROUPS`) checked before and after.

## Conclusion

The hard part of cooling anomaly detection is not the detection algorithm. EWMA, CUSUM and a regression residual are enough, and the benchmarks say fancier methods rarely beat them. The hard part is everything around the algorithm: knowing which rack a CRAH actually cools, telling a drifting thermistor from a fouling coil, collapsing a cascade into one root, keeping advisories out of the pager, and proving any of it works. The industry has no independent answer to that last question. LBNL calls accuracy a "considerable uncertainty", and every datacenter vendor claim is unevaluated. A simulator with a fault ledger, publish-layer sensor faults and ground-truth air-influence weights is therefore not just a test rig for this platform. It is the only way this design can produce the lead-time and false-alarm numbers the market does not publish.

On AI, the answer reverses the usual framing. The system needs statistics and physics everywhere, a little classical machine learning in two places (baselines and the rack-to-CRAH influence fit), and an LLM nowhere on the critical path. An LLM becomes worth adding only once incidents are already correctly grouped and evidenced. At that point it is a cheap, optional narrator over facts the platform has already established, and the thing to measure is whether operators trust the evidence panel, not the prose.
